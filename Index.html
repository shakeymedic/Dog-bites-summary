Dog Bite Management in the ED

A practical clinical and legal reference for UK Emergency Medicine Doctors. Based on RCEM Guidelines, DFTB, and Dr Vicky Currie's clinical review.

The Reporting Golden Rule

You have no statutory obligation to report all dog bite injuries to the police.

Patient confidentiality remains your primary duty under GMC and Data Protection Act rules. You must make reporting decisions on a case-by-case basis. You should report if the bite links to a crime or presents an ongoing public safety risk.

Epidemiology & Risk Factors

  • England sees over 10,000 hospital admissions for dog bites annually. Children make up roughly 25% of these cases.
  • 75% of bites occur in the three most deprived socioeconomic deciles.
  • Paediatric injuries involve the face or head 50% of the time.
  • Red Flag: You will frequently see severe dog bites in families with existing social care involvement or a history of domestic violence.

The Legal Framework

The Dangerous Dogs Act applies to any dog dangerously out of control in any place. The UK bans four specific dog types:

Japanese Tosa
Dogo Argentino
Brazilian Mastiff
XL Bully Type

Owners must meet five criteria to keep a banned breed legally:

  • The dog must be neutered.
  • The dog must carry a microchip.
  • The owner must keep the dog in a secure enclosure.
  • The owner must hold third-party insurance.
  • The owner must hold a Certificate of Exemption.

ED Clinical Management

1. Initial Stabilisation

  • Apply standard C-ABC protocols. Control catastrophic haemorrhage first.
  • Cover the wound with a clean non-adhesive dressing immediately.
  • Remove all jewellery promptly. Swelling will cause ischaemia.
  • Provide rapid analgesia. Use intranasal fentanyl, diamorphine, or pentazocine.

2. Wound Care

  • Irrigate thoroughly with high-pressure saline. Create pressure using a bag of saline and a cut intravenous catheter.
  • Refer large wounds for an operative washout in theatre.
  • Close wounds under 12 hours old at most sites. You can close facial wounds up to 24 hours old.
  • Leave older wounds open to heal.
  • Lower your threshold for plastic surgery referral for bites to the head, face, hands, feet, tendons, or joints.

3. Antibiotics & Vaccines

  • Do not prescribe routine antibiotics for every bite.
  • Prescribe antibiotics for deep punctures, crush injuries, contamination, delayed presentations, or bites to high-risk areas.
  • First Line: Co-amoxiclav 500/125mg three times daily.
  • Penicillin Allergy: Doxycycline 100mg twice daily.
  • Severe Infection: IV Co-amoxiclav 1.2g eight hourly.
  • Check tetanus status. Booster adults at 10 years or 5 years for contaminated wounds. Give immunoglobulin for high-risk cases. UK rabies risk is negligible; consult the Green Book for travel exposures.

Safeguarding Children

  • Judge each case individually based on the history and context.
  • Refer automatically if the child already has social care involvement. These children represent a significantly higher risk profile.
  • Distinguish between provoked and unprovoked attacks. Unprovoked attacks raise major concerns regarding dog temperament and environmental control.
  • Ask parents for consent to disclose information. You can still share details with appropriate authorities if they refuse.

Check Your Biases

Clinical judgement heavily influences referral rates. Be aware of common cognitive biases when assessing risk.

Breed Bias

Clinicians often under-report bites from familiar or "friendly" breeds like spaniels compared to large breeds like XL Bullies. You must treat every dog as a potential risk to children.

Ownership Bias

Clinicians who own dogs themselves often have a lower threshold for specialist referral and reporting than those who do not.

Test Your Knowledge: Clinical Scenarios

Scenario: A 5-year-old girl suffers facial lacerations from the family dog. The dog is a non-banned breed they have owned for its whole life. There is no deep structural damage. The family opposes police involvement.

Action: Washout in theatre and admit under plastic surgery. Provide analgesia and antibiotics. Despite the family's opposition and the dog being a non-banned breed, you must still refer this to police and social care due to the unprovoked facial injury on a young child.

Scenario: A 5-year-old boy is attacked on the road by two unrestrained XL Bully dogs. He sustains deep lacerations to his axilla, thigh, and arm. His CT abdomen is negative.

Action: Resuscitate, provide IV antibiotics, and perform a theatre washout with plastic surgery. The police must be involved immediately as XL Bullies are a banned breed and they present a clear, ongoing public safety risk. Make an urgent social care referral due to the severity of the incident.

Scenario: A 2-year-old girl attends with a superficial foot wound. She stepped on the paw of the sleeping family dog. The dog has no history of biting. There are no previous ED attendances for anyone in the household.

Action: Irrigate the wound and discharge. Do not make a safeguarding referral. The mechanism fits the injury; the attack was provoked, there is no history of concern, and there are no other red flags.
